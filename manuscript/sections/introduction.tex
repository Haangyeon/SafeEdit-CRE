Functional assays such as MPRA and increasingly capable sequence-to-activity models
have moved CRE research from retrospective annotation toward predictive sequence
design~\citep{Melnikov2012,Arnold2013,Tewhey2016,Inoue2017,Gordon2020,Kircher2019,
Avsec2021,Taskiran2024,Gosai2024}. The next engineering step is not simply to find a
higher-scoring sequence: it is to turn these predictions into a small number of
traceable constructs that can be synthesized, compared, and iterated in the laboratory.
That step remains difficult because model scores do not by themselves indicate whether
an edit is robust across predictors or practical to build. CREs are particularly well
suited to this bridge between biology and engineering: their transcription-factor
binding sites and surrounding sequence context encode when and where genes are
expressed~\citep{Wasserman2004,ENCODE2012,Thurman2012,Roadmap2015,Sahu2022}, while
their compact length permits direct synthesis and reporter measurement. A reliable
design workflow could therefore support both mechanistic tests of cell-type-selective
regulatory grammar and the development of tissue-selective control elements for
synthetic biology, gene delivery, and functional noncoding-variant prioritization.

Current regulatory-design strategies have largely emphasized de novo generation or
direct maximization of a single predictor~\citep{Vaishnav2022,Almeida2024,Gosai2024}.
They can identify high-scoring sequences, but may alter large parts of a construct or
leave the natural CRE context entirely. The resulting candidates are harder to
attribute to specific nucleotide changes, increase synthesis and screening burden, and
provide less guidance for the next design cycle. For experimental and engineering
workflows, a more useful output is a compact panel of traceable variants that preserves
the parent scaffold and changes only a defined number of nucleotides.

We define this setting as \emph{minimal editing}: increasing cell-type selectivity while
retaining nearly all of the parent CRE. The parent sequence preserves native context,
whereas a fixed edit budget controls design distance, construct burden, and the number
of causal changes that must be interpreted. Each variant is therefore a controlled
perturbation rather than an unrelated synthetic sequence. The output is explicit,
auditable, and directly transferable to oligonucleotide synthesis and reporter
screening, making minimal editing a practical interface between model ranking and
experimental iteration.

Minimal changes alone, however, do not guarantee reliable designs. A single-predictor
search can reward model-specific behavior even when the predictor performs well on
held-out natural sequences~\citep{Whalen2022,deBoer2024,Ovadia2019,Rafi2025}. Greedy
optimization retains substitutions for their immediate gain under one objective;
wider beam search explores more paths but remains vulnerable when the same model
generates and ranks every candidate. The unresolved problem is therefore selection:
identifying a compact set of interpretable edits whose predicted effects persist across
separately trained, architecture-diverse models while uncertainty and sequence-domain
behavior remain controlled. This is the point at which a predictive model becomes an
engineering decision system rather than a score generator.

We developed SafeEdit-CRE to solve this selection problem (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}).
The published Malinois predictor~\citep{Gosai2024} supplies the primary cell-type
specificity objective. During constrained beam search, an architecture-diverse
residual-dilated CNN ensemble reviews proposed variants, while validation-calibrated
uncertainty and safeguards for strand consistency, GC shift, homopolymers, and 6-mer
naturalness filter unstable or sequence-atypical edits. A second, separately trained
multi-kernel ensemble is excluded from candidate generation and used only for final
cross-model evaluation. This procedural separation of search, review, and evaluation
makes model agreement an explicit design criterion rather than a retrospective check.
Fixed budgets of 1, 5, 10, and 20 substitutions further allow design freedom to be
quantified without conflating minimal editing with unconstrained generation.

We benchmarked SafeEdit-CRE against random substitutions, iterative greedy editing,
and a compute-matched primary-model beam with the same beam width and expansion
schedule. Evaluation comprised cross-model generalization on 600 held-out natural CRE
parents across K562, HepG2, and SK-N-SH cells; a locked nine-criterion design-quality
benchmark on 90 non-overlapping parents; cell-type- and budget-stratified analyses; and
complete re-search ablations. SafeEdit-CRE increased cross-model specificity margin
over greedy editing, maintained closely matched predicted specificity against the
compute-matched beam while lowering ensemble uncertainty, and raised the locked
design-audit pass rate from 38.8\% to 60.6\%. The resulting Tier A library contains 74
variants across all three cell types and four edit budgets. Thus, the framework
contributes both a stringent test of cross-model minimal editing and a directly usable
engineering output: a compact, traceable, reporter-screening-ready panel that
concentrates synthesis and experimental resources on higher-confidence CRE designs.
In practical terms, it reduces the number of constructs that must be ordered and
screened, while retaining enough sequence context to interpret why each candidate
changes cell-type selectivity.
